#Abstract

libsdca is an open-source library of classification losses for training neural networks and a stochastic dual coordinate ascent (SDCA) solver for linear and kernel models. We implement fused GPU and CPU kernels for PyTorch and JAX, and test loss values and gradients against high-precision oracle references. Our implementation is numerically accurate and fast. We maintain correct cross-entropy (CE) gradients at 4–⁠8 times higher margins while being faster than compiled PyTorch and JAX in most comparisons. The previously observed loss spikes at high confidence and the drift of standard CE disappear in our experiments, and we achieve a similar effect with a multiclass hinge loss, which groks modular arithmetic at margins near one. We also provide fused kernels for the top-\(k\) and proximal losses. They are several times faster and use a fraction of the memory compared to the natural baseline of composed tensor operations. On fixed features, our SDCA solver fits multiclass SVMs up to 20 times faster than LIBLINEAR on most problems we tested and uses the duality gap as a well-defined stopping criterion.

#Introduction

Cross-entropy gradients become small as a classifier becomes confident in the correct class. The standard formula can round the target gradient to zero while it is still large enough to be represented. Prieto et al. (2025) named this effect softmax collapse and showed that it can prevent grokking (Power et al., 2022). Barron et al. (2026) reformulated cross-entropy to avoid the collapse and gave a similar fix for softmax cancellation in distillation. We call their formulas fixed CE, as opposed to the standard unfixed CE.

PyTorch 2.15 and JAX 0.12.1 still suffer from these numerical issues (Table 2). Even a straightforward implementation of fixed CE can lose gradients when there are many classes or when mixed-precision training scales the loss (Section 2.2). Fixing these issues is non-trivial as efficiency matters in practice. A loss written as a sequence of tensor operations creates intermediate arrays, reducing the speed and increasing the peak memory use.

libsdca addresses both problems with fused kernels: GPU and CPU routines that write the loss and its gradient without intermediate arrays. We compute the exponentials in single precision, accumulate the sums of each row in double precision, and round every output once. This keeps the small gradients and adds little work, allowing us to be faster than the built-in losses in most comparisons. On large inputs, our CE kernel takes about as long as reading the logits and writing the gradient to memory (Section 3).

The library provides cross-entropy, multiclass hinge, top-\(k\), multilabel, distillation, and proximal losses for PyTorch and JAX. It also has an SDCA solver for linear and kernel models on fixed features (Section 1). To try our fixed cross-entropy on an existing model, change the import as follows.

# PyTorch: in place of torch.nn.CrossEntropyLoss
from libsdca.torch import CrossEntropyLoss
# JAX: in place of the Optax function of the same name
from libsdca.optax import softmax_cross_entropy_with_integer_labels

Both interfaces accept the same arguments as their framework counterparts, and run as fast as the compiled loss or faster (Figure 1). Our main contributions are summarized below.

On the benchmark inputs, a paired sign test finds the gradients of libsdca more accurate than those of the baseline in 97 of 180 comparisons and less accurate in 5.
  • Our fused kernels are fast (Section 3). The difference must also exceed 3% (Section 3.2). The standard frameworks have no optimized kernels for the top-\(k\) losses and the smooth multiclass hinge, and so we compare with their natural baseline, a composition of tensor operations. With many classes, our kernels are 6–⁠28 times faster (all 36 significant) and need 3–⁠18 times less memory.
  • Numerical accuracy matters in training (Section 4). We reproduce the grokking and distillation experiments of Barron et al. (2026), and observe that the loss spikes and the drift of standard CE disappear with our layers. A multiclass hinge and top-\(k\) losses also grok modular arithmetic, and the hinges do so at margins near one, which avoids the issues of tiny gradients.
  • The SDCA solver is accurate and fast (Section 1.2, Appendices B.4 and D.4). Our solver reports a fit as solved only when the duality gap confirms the requested tolerance. To our knowledge, both this observation and the grokking results for multiclass hinge and top-\(k\) losses are new. #1. The libsdca library

libsdca provides loss layers for neural networks in PyTorch and JAX, with fused kernels for CUDA and CPU. It also has an SDCA solver for the CPU to train linear and kernel classifiers on fixed features, and offers interfaces for NumPy, Rust, C, C++11, and scikit-learn.

#1.1 Loss layers and notation

Let \(z\in\mathbb R^m\) be the class scores (logits) of an example and \(y\) its true class, the target. The other classes \(j\ne y\) are the competitors, and \(a_j=z_j-z_y\) are their score differences. The margin is \(g=z_y-\max_{j\ne y}z_j\). It is positive for a correct prediction and grows with its confidence.

The base single-label classification losses are cross-entropy and the multiclass hinge (Crammer & Singer, 2001):

\[ \ell_{\mathrm{CE}}(z,y)=\log\Bigl(1+\sum_{j\ne y}e^{a_j}\Bigr),\qquad \ell_{\mathrm{hinge}}(z,y)=\max\Bigl(0,\,1+\max_{j\ne y}a_j\Bigr).\]
(1)

Unlike the hinge loss, which is zero once the margin reaches 1, cross-entropy is positive at every finite margin and keeps pushing well-classified logits apart. This is why CE needs accurate arithmetic at large margins (Section 2), while the hinge does not reward large margins and can grok without them (Section 4.2). A smoothing parameter \(\gamma>0\) replaces the kink of the hinge with a quadratic piece and makes its gradient continuous.

Top-\(k\) and other losses. When categories overlap, several labels may fit an example even if only one is considered correct. Requiring that label to rank first can be too strict. The library also has multilabel cross-entropy and hinge losses for label sets, and knowledge distillation against teacher logits (Hinton et al., 2015). The proximal losses are smoothed forms of cross-entropy and distillation with a step \(\gamma>0\). They are built like the smooth hinge (Appendix B). Every layer in Table 1 is called as follows.

loss = TopKCrossEntropyLoss(k=5)(logits, labels)
loss = DistillationLoss(temperature=2.0)(student_logits, teacher_logits)

#1.2 Certified SDCA solver

On fixed features such as frozen pretrained embeddings, fitting a linear or kernel classifier with cross-entropy, the hinges, or the top-\(k\) and multilabel losses is a convex problem. Given \(X\in\mathbb R^{n\times d}\) and a regularization parameter \(C>0\), libsdca minimizes

\[ P(W)=\frac12\lVert W\rVert_F^2+C\sum_{i=1}^n\ell(W^\top x_i,\,y_i)\]
(2)

by stochastic dual coordinate ascent (SDCA; Shalev-Shwartz & Zhang, 2013). It maximizes the dual \(D(A)\) one example at a time, with \(W=X^\top A\) (Appendix B.4).

The duality gap \(P(W)-D(A)\) is an upper bound on the suboptimality of \(W\) at any iterate. The solver reports status solved only when the relative gap is below the requested tolerance. We call such a solution certified. The solver works on the features \(X\) or on a Gram or kernel matrix \(K=XX^\top\) that the user passes as libsdca.Kernel(K). The second form suits \(d>n\), and \(K\) can be reused across fits. Its entry points are libsdca.fit and libsdca.sklearn.SDCAClassifier.

#2.1 Floating-point background

A floating-point format has limited precision and limited range. When two numbers differ greatly in magnitude, their sum can round to the larger one. A result whose magnitude is less than half the smallest positive number in the format rounds to zero (underflow). Table C.1 gives both limits for the four formats of this report: fp16, bf16, fp32, and fp64.

For cross-entropy, let \(\tau\) be the exponential weight of all competitors relative to the target:

\[ \tau=\sum_{j\ne y}e^{z_j-z_y},\qquad \ell=\log(1+\tau),\qquad \frac{\partial\ell}{\partial z_y}=-\frac{\tau}{1+\tau}.\]
(3)

The weight \(\tau\) decays exponentially as the margin grows. Unfixed implementations compute \(1+\tau\). Once \(\tau\) is below the precision of the format, this sum rounds to \(1\). The loss and the target gradient are then exactly zero (softmax collapse; Prieto et al., 2025).

Collapse happens long before \(\tau\) underflows. In fp32 with two classes, the standard formula returns zero beyond a margin of 16.6. The format can represent the gradient up to a margin of 104 (Table C.4). Even before collapse, the computed gradient is about 8% too large at margin 14 and 17% too large at margin 15. Cross-entropy continues to reward larger margins, so training can bring confident predictions into this range.

Knowledge distillation has a related problem near the teacher. Its gradient is a difference of two nearly equal probabilities. Subtracting them after rounding leaves mostly rounding noise (cancellation).

#2.2 Collapse-free formulations and implementation

Barron et al. (2026) evaluate cross-entropy directly from \(\tau\) (Equation 3) and compute the probability difference for distillation in log space. Our kernels use both formulations and reproduce their grokking and distillation results (Section 4.1).

Fixed CE can preserve a small gradient until the gradient itself underflows. Whether it does so depends on how the kernel sums the competitor terms and when it rounds the gradient. Both choices matter most in fp16 because its range is narrow.

Tail underflow. With many classes, every term \(e^{z_j-z_y}\) can underflow to zero while the sum of the terms is still representable. We observe that one of the reference listings of fixed CE sums the unshifted terms and underflows (Appendix C.1). In contrast, the standard logsumexp shifts by the largest competitor and does not underflow.

Premature rounding. Mixed-precision training multiplies the loss by a large scale so that small gradients survive in fp16 (Micikevicius et al., 2018). This works only if the scale is applied before the gradient is rounded. A kernel that first stores the unscaled gradient in fp16 loses the small entries. We measure this in the cross-entropy kernel of Liger-Kernel (Hsu et al., 2024), a widely used library of fused kernels (Appendix C.2).

Our fused kernels read the logits in place and keep the sums of each row in double precision. They apply the shift and the scale to these sums and round each gradient entry once. No extra pass over the logits is needed (Section 3).

#2.3 Cancellation in hinge losses

Multiclass hinges contain no exponentials, but each competitor term \(h_j=1+z_j-z_y\) still adds numbers of different scale. If one score is tiny and the other score is near 1, plain addition drops the low-order bits of the tiny score. It can then return zero for a small positive term, an effect similar to softmax collapse. No fixed order of the two additions avoids this on all inputs (Appendix C.3).

We evaluate \(h_j\) with Knuth's error-free TwoSum (Ogita et al., 2005). It returns the rounded sum \(s=\mathrm{fl}(u+v)\) and its exact rounding error \(e=(u+v)-s\):

\[ (d,e_1)=\mathrm{TwoSum}(z_j,-z_y),\qquad (s,e_2)=\mathrm{TwoSum}(d,1),\qquad h_j=s+(e_1+e_2). \]

Adding back both errors keeps the small hinge terms and the gradients near the unit margin. To our knowledge, this cancellation between tiny scores and the unit margin has not been reported before.

#2.4 Numerical verification

We compare every loss value and gradient entry with a high-precision mpmath reference in all four floating-point formats (Appendix C.4). Our tests cover inputs for each effect of Table 2, margin sweeps, and loss-scaled fp16 batches, both on GPU and CPU.

Our cross-entropy follows the exact gradient until it underflows, while PyTorch F.cross_entropy returns zero starting from the collapse margin (Figure 2). In the grokking experiment of Section 4, F.cross_entropy loses the gradients of up to 85% of the training rows of \(a+b\bmod 31\) in fp32. libsdca loses none (Figure 5, Appendix E.2).

Property tests in the style of Maaz (2026) and a scan of each parameter to the end of each format find the limits listed in Appendix C.5. On the timed inputs of the benchmark in Section 3, no output of libsdca has a nonfinite entry or a zero in place of a nonzero value. A paired sign test finds the gradients of libsdca more accurate than those of eager PyTorch, torch.compile, and jax.jit in 97 of 180 comparisons and less accurate in 5 (Appendix D.3). All 5 are bf16 cross-entropy with probability targets or with class weights and label smoothing, on 32,000 and 50,257 classes. A few of their smallest gradient entries are less accurate, but the error of every entry stays below the rounding error of bf16 relative to the largest entry of its row, as in the baseline.

#3. Runtime and memory

The numerical safeguards of Section 2 do not make our losses slower. In training, our cross-entropy is as fast as compiled PyTorch or faster on large inputs and about twice as fast on small ones. The largest savings in time and memory come from fusing losses that would otherwise require several tensor operations.

#3.1 Kernel design

With many classes, memory traffic accounts for most of the time spent computing a loss such as cross-entropy. Our kernels therefore read and write each array as few times as possible and keep the rows they work on in registers. We write the CUDA kernels in Triton (Tillet et al., 2019) and the CPU kernels in vectorized OpenMP code. Four design choices explain where they are faster than the baselines.3Table D.1 gives the GPU time of the calls behind them.

  • One read per pass. The forward pass reads each row once and keeps a few sums of it. Rows of up to 4096 classes stay in registers. Wider rows are split into chunks that run in parallel, and their sums are merged in fp64. The backward pass reads the row once more and writes its gradient. Eager PyTorch and jax.jit make more passes over the logits (Table D.1).
  • Whole-vector loads at any width. A GPU reads memory fastest in aligned 16-byte vectors. A row of 50,257 bf16 logits is 100,514 bytes long, so most rows start between two 16-byte boundaries. Our chunks start at the boundary at or below the start of the row. Every chunk then loads whole vectors, and the first and last chunk of a row mask the bytes outside it. On \(2560\times50{,}257\) bf16 logits, our kernels take 0.61 ms of GPU time and those of compiled PyTorch 0.86 ms.
  • No sort in the top-\(k\) and hinge losses. Our kernels keep the \(k\) largest scores of a row in registers and solve the projections of Appendix B on these candidates. The compositions call topk or sort every row. A hinge gradient has only a few nonzero entries per row, so on wide rows the backward pass writes zeros to the other chunks without reading them.
  • Less CPU work per call. On small inputs, the CPU work of launching the kernels dominates the time of a call. We launch ours from C++, while a compiled PyTorch call also runs Python guards, wrappers, and launchers.

The precision of the internal arithmetic is the working precision. By default, the exponentials run in fp32 for 16- and 32-bit logits, and the row sums run in fp64.

#3.2 Benchmark experiments

We time the loss layers on NVIDIA A100 GPUs and an AMD EPYC 7J13 CPU against eager PyTorch, torch.compile (Ansel et al., 2024), and jax.jit. The inputs have 9 to 128,256 classes in bf16 and fp32. We time training calls, which run the forward and the backward pass, and forward-only calls. The baselines are the built-in losses of the frameworks and our compositions of tensor operations for the losses that have none. A speedup is the time of the baseline over ours, so above 1 libsdca is faster. The time per logit is the time of a call divided by its rows times classes. libsdca is faster or slower only when a one-sided \(t\)-test on the log speedups of four to six fresh processes is significant at \(q<0.05\) after a Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995) and the difference exceeds 3% (Appendix D.1).

Built-in losses. Against the built-in losses under torch.compile and jax.jit, libsdca is faster in 403 of 519 GPU speed comparisons and slower in 29 (Figure 3). Our CE and that of compiled PyTorch both reach the memory-bandwidth limit on large inputs with aligned rows (Figure 1). Ours is 1.9–⁠2.3 times faster on about a million logits and 1.45 times on bf16 rows that are not a multiple of 16 bytes. A compiled training step takes the same time with either loss. On the CPU, libsdca is faster in 29 of 32 comparisons and slower only for fp32 CE in JAX on \(256\times10\) logits (Appendix D.2).

Losses without a built-in kernel. For losses such as top-5 cross-entropy, the top-5 hinges, and the smooth multiclass hinge, the baseline is a compiled composition of tensor operations, which writes and reads several arrays of the size of the logits. In training with 8193 classes or more, libsdca is 5.9–⁠11.5 times faster on the top-5 losses and 14–⁠28 times faster on the smooth hinge (all 36 significant). In PyTorch training on \(4096\times32{,}000\) logits, the composition needs 3–⁠18 times more memory (Figure 1, Appendix D.2).

SDCA solver. The solver fits most of the multiclass SVM problems that we tested faster than LIBLINEAR (Figure 4). Its kernel form with a reused Gram matrix is fastest on wide data (Appendix D.4).

#4. Training experiments

We now show that our numerically accurate cross-entropy avoids the loss spikes at high confidence on modular arithmetic tasks, and that a multiclass hinge reaches the test accuracy of fixed CE at margins near 1.

We chose tasks that exhibit grokking (Power et al., 2022): the model memorizes the training set long before it generalizes, and can become very confident on the training examples during that time. Following Varma et al. (2023) and Barron et al. (2026), we train a 1-layer ReLU transformer on \(a+b \bmod 31\) with full-batch AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019) and weight decay. We use ten random seeds and vary AdamW's \(\epsilon\) to let the margins grow further toward the collapse threshold of Section 2.1 (Appendix E.1).

#4.1 Cross-entropy and distillation

As training progresses, the margins of unfixed CE climb to the collapse threshold of about 16.6 in fp32. At that point, most training rows collapse, the margins fall, and the loss spikes (Figure 5). This cycle repeats, and the final test accuracy stays a few points below that of the fixed losses (Table E.1).

In contrast, there are no collapses with fixed CE and our CE loss. The margins climb smoothly to about \(g^\star=\log(1/\epsilon)\), at which point the gradients are as small as \(\epsilon\) and the AdamW steps shrink. The test accuracies of the two losses show no significant difference across seeds in bf16, fp32, and fp64, and our top-2 and proximal cross-entropy losses train just as smoothly (Appendix E.2).

Two other remedies end below the test accuracy of fixed CE (Table E.1): StableMax (Prieto et al., 2025) in every setting, and zero-sum gradient projection (Liu et al., 2026) at the two smaller values of \(\epsilon\). Unfixed CE also collapses on three further modular arithmetic tasks, while fixed CE and the libsdca losses remain stable (Appendix E.3).

Distillation. To isolate the effect of rounding in distillation, we follow Barron et al. (2026) and initialize a student MLP with its teacher's weights on \(a+b\bmod 53\). We train the student with Adam (Kingma & Ba, 2015) at temperature \(T=4\). Since the student already matches the teacher, the exact gradient is zero. However, unfixed CE introduces rounding noise in the gradients, which moves the student away from the teacher. The drift is small in fp32, but the student loses most of its test accuracy in bf16 arithmetic. Fixed CE and our distillation layers return exact zero gradients. The student keeps its initial weights throughout training in bf16, fp32, and fp64 (Figure 6, Appendix E.4).

#4.2 Grokking with a multiclass hinge

An example stops contributing to the multiclass hinge loss once its margin reaches 1. Interestingly, grokking can still happen when training with weight decay because it keeps shrinking the weights even after memorization. The hinge loss then acts only on the examples that fall back below the margin.

Trained with weight decay on \(a+b\bmod 31\), the multiclass hinge reaches the test accuracy of our cross-entropy at all three values of \(\epsilon\) (Table 3). Its final margins stay near 1.2, while CE ends at margins of 18 to 37. Almost all training examples end at exactly zero loss. The top-3 hinges (Table E.1) and the hinges on the three further tasks behave alike.

#5. Conclusion

libsdca provides classification losses that are numerically accurate and fast, and an SDCA solver that uses the duality gap as a well-defined stopping criterion. Our CE loss keeps the small gradients that the built-in loss returns as zero, and it trains as fast as compiled PyTorch or faster. Our solver fits most of the multiclass SVM problems that we tested faster than LIBLINEAR. In our grokking runs, a multiclass hinge reaches the test accuracy of fixed CE at margins near one.

We expect accurate losses to matter most late in training, when confident predictions approach the collapse margin (Section 2.1), and in distillation, when the student closely matches its teacher. However, an accurate loss alone does not change how a model rounds its logits (Chen et al., 2026).

Limitations. We leave three points for future work.

  • Scale of the experiments. The training runs cover four modular-arithmetic tasks with small models, and only the loss runs in the stated precision. The half-precision effects of Section 2.2 are measured on gradients, not in large-scale fp16 training.
  • Numerical limits. Individual losses may still lose accuracy or return NaN near the largest finite number, in the subnormal range, or with very large class weights. Cross-entropy with probability targets also loses accuracy on rows with tied top scores (Appendix C.5).
  • Speed and memory. libsdca is slower than the compiled built-in loss in 29 of 519 GPU speed comparisons, with a lowest speedup of 0.48. Most are distillation and multilabel cross-entropy in JAX and forward-only cross-entropy, and with few classes libsdca may need more memory than compiled PyTorch (Appendix D.2). The torch.compile baselines run without CUDA graphs, which reduce the host work on small inputs.

#Appendix B. Losses and the SDCA solver

This appendix defines the losses of Table 1, derives the formulas that our kernels evaluate, and states the problem for the SDCA solver. Appendix B.1 derives the top-\(k\) losses, Appendix B.2 writes every loss as a maximum over gradient candidates, Appendix B.3 lists all losses in this common form, and Appendix B.4 uses this form to obtain the dual problem and the stopping criterion of the solver.

Notation. As in Section 1.1, \(z\in\mathbb R^m\) are the scores, \(y\) is the target, and \(a_j=z_j-z_y\) are the differences of the competitors \(j\ne y\). We sort the differences and write the competitor weight \(\tau\) as in Equation 3:

\[ a_{[1]}\ge a_{[2]}\ge\dots\ge a_{[m-1]},\qquad \tau=\sum_{j\ne y}e^{a_j}. \]

\(H(q)=-\sum_i q_i\log q_i\) is the entropy, and \(\Delta_n\) is the set of probability distributions in \(\mathbb R^n\).

#B.1 Top-\(k\) losses

When categories overlap, an example can fit several labels. The top-\(k\) error then counts a prediction as correct when the target is among the \(k\) highest scores.

Top-\(k\) hinges. The multiclass hinge loss of Equation 1 can be generalized for the top-\(k\) error in two ways (Lapin et al., 2016): variant \(\alpha\) applies the hinge to the average of the \(k\) largest differences, and variant \(\beta\) averages \(k\) separate hinges.

\[ \ell_\alpha(z,y)=\max\Bigl(0,\,1+\frac1k\sum_{i=1}^k a_{[i]}\Bigr),\qquad \ell_\beta(z,y)=\frac1k\sum_{i=1}^k\max\bigl(0,\,1+a_{[i]}\bigr).\]
(B.1)

\(\ell_\alpha\) is zero if the \(k\) largest competitors are on average at least 1 below the target, while \(\ell_\beta\) is zero if every competitor is at least 1 below the target. Both upper bound the top-\(k\) error, and both are \(\geq 1\) if the target is not among the \(k\) highest scores. It can be shown that each gradient entry is capped at \(1/k\), which gives a recipe for the top-\(k\) cross-entropy below.

Top-\(k\) cross-entropy. Cross-entropy has a variational form. For any vector \(b\in\mathbb R^n\), the log-sum-exp is the largest value of a linear term plus an entropy, and the maximizer is the softmax (Boyd & Vandenberghe, 2004):

\[ \log\sum_i e^{b_i}=\max_{q\in\Delta_n}\Bigl[\sum_i q_ib_i+H(q)\Bigr],\qquad q^\star_i=\frac{e^{b_i}}{\sum_l e^{b_l}}.\]
(B.2)

Applied to the competitor differences, Equation B.2 puts the weight \(q_j\) on competitor \(j\). Correspondingly, top-\(k\) cross-entropy caps each weight at \(1/k\) (Lapin et al., 2016):

\[ \ell_{\mathrm{top}k}(z,y)=\log\bigl(1+e^{\omega_k}\bigr),\qquad \omega_k=\max_{q\in\Delta_{m-1},\;q_j\le 1/k}\Bigl[\sum_{j\ne y}q_ja_j+H(q)\Bigr].\]
(B.3)

At \(k=1\), we have \(\omega_1=\log\tau\) by Equation B.2, and the loss is the standard cross-entropy of Equation 1. Appendix B.2 shows where the outer \(\log(1+e^{\omega})\) comes from.

Closed form. The cap makes Equation B.3 a constrained problem, but its solution is simple. The optimality conditions cut each softmax weight at the cap, with a normalizer \(\nu\) that makes the weights sum to one:

\[ q^\star_j=\min\bigl(1/k,\;e^{a_j-\nu}\bigr). \]

Some number \(u\) of the largest competitors reach the cap. For \(k<m-1\), it holds that \(u<k\), because \(k\) capped weights would already sum to one while every weight is positive. At \(k=m-1\), every weight is \(1/k\), and \(\omega_k\) is \(\log k\) plus the mean of the differences. The other weights sum to \((k-u)/k\), which fixes \(\nu\) through the log-sum-exp \(L_u\) of the competitors below the cap. With \(a_{[0]}=+\infty\), the cap separates the sorted differences at the index \(u\) with

\[ L_u=\log\sum_{i>u}e^{a_{[i]}},\qquad \nu=L_u-\log\frac{k-u}{k},\qquad a_{[u]}>L_u-\log(k-u)\ge a_{[u+1]}.\]
(B.4)

Substituting \(q^\star\) into Equation B.3 gives (Lapin et al., 2018, Proposition 17)

\[ \omega_k=\log k+\frac1k\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^u a_{[i]}+(k-u)\bigl(L_u-\log(k-u)\bigr)\Bigr).\]
(B.5)

Gradient. By Appendix B.2, the gradient is \(q^\star\) times the sigmoid \(s^\star=1/(1+e^{-\omega_k})\):

\[ \frac{\partial\ell_{\mathrm{top}k}}{\partial z_j}=s^\star q^\star_j= \begin{cases} s^\star/k, & j\text{ among the }u\text{ largest},\\ s^\star\,\dfrac{k-u}{k}\,e^{a_j-L_u}, & \text{otherwise}, \end{cases} \qquad \frac{\partial\ell_{\mathrm{top}k}}{\partial z_y}=-s^\star.\]
(B.6)

No competitor receives more than \(1/k\) of the gradient. A loss that accepts \(k\) labels therefore does not push a single plausible competitor down with full force. The kernel needs only the target score, the \(k\) largest competitors, and the log-sum-exp of the rest. One pass over the row computes all three.

#B.2 Losses as maxima over gradients

Every loss of libsdca is a maximum of linear functions of the scores. This form gives the gradient without differentiating a formula and makes every gradient row sum to zero. It also turns smoothing into a small change of one term, and it supplies the dual variables of the SDCA solver (Appendix B.4).

General form. For a convex set \(\Theta\) of gradient candidates and a convex function \(\Psi\) on it, a loss of libsdca has the form

\[ \ell(z)=\max_{\theta\in\Theta}\bigl[\langle\theta,z\rangle-\Psi(\theta)\bigr], \qquad \nabla\ell(z)=\theta^\star.\]
(B.7)

The function \(\Psi\) is the convex conjugate of \(\ell\) (Boyd & Vandenberghe, 2004). A maximum of linear functions is convex. When the maximizer \(\theta^\star\) is unique, it is the gradient by Danskin's theorem. Every candidate of libsdca sums to zero, so every gradient row sums to zero as well. For a single-label loss, the target entry is therefore \(\theta_y=-\sum_{j\ne y}\theta_j\), minus the total weight of the competitors. Our kernels compute it from this sum and never as \(p_y-1\) with a rounded \(p_y\) close to 1, the step that collapses in a standard implementation (Section 2.1).

Single-label losses. Let a candidate put weights \(p_j\ge0\) on the competitors and \(-s\) on the target, with \(s=\sum_{j\ne y}p_j\). Then \(\langle\theta,z\rangle=\sum_{j\ne y}p_ja_j\), and Equation B.7 becomes

\[ \ell(z,y)=\max_{p\in\mathcal C}\Bigl[\sum_{j\ne y}p_j(c+a_j)-\Omega(p)\Bigr]\]
(B.8)

with an offset \(c\), a regularizer \(\Omega\), and one of three feasible sets:

\[ \Delta=\{p\ge0:s\le1\},\qquad \Delta_k^\alpha=\{p\in\Delta:p_j\le s/k\},\qquad \Delta_k^\beta=\{p\in\Delta:p_j\le 1/k\}.\]
(B.9)

The hinges have \(c=1\) and \(\Omega=0\). A linear function attains its maximum over \(\Delta\) at a vertex. That is either \(p=0\), with loss zero, or a single competitor with weight one, which gives the hinge of Equation 1. The vertices of \(\Delta_k^\alpha\) spread the weight \(1/k\) over \(k\) competitors, and those of \(\Delta_k^\beta\) over up to \(k\) competitors. They give Equation B.1.

Cross-entropy has \(c=0\) and \(\Omega(p)=-H(\bar p)\), where \(\bar p=(1-s,p)\) is a distribution over all \(m\) classes. Equation B.2 with \(b=(0,a)\) then gives Equation 1, and the maximizer is the softmax. Top-\(k\) cross-entropy uses the same \(\Omega\) on \(\Delta_k^\alpha\). Writing \(p=s\,q\) with \(q\in\Delta_{m-1}\) turns the cap \(p_j\le s/k\) into \(q_j\le1/k\) and splits the entropy as \(H(\bar p)=h(s)+s\,H(q)\), with the binary entropy \(h\). The maximum over \(q\) is \(\omega_k\) of Equation B.3, and the maximum over \(s\) is Equation B.2 with \(b=(0,\omega_k)\):

\[ \max_{s\in[0,1]}\bigl[s\,\omega_k+h(s)\bigr]=\log\bigl(1+e^{\omega_k}\bigr),\qquad s^\star=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\omega_k}}. \]

This gives the outer \(\log(1+e^{\omega_k})\) of Equation B.3 and the gradient of Equation B.6.

Smoothing. Adding a quadratic to \(\Psi\) smooths the loss (Nesterov, 2005). The result is the Moreau envelope of \(\ell\) with step \(\gamma\) (Parikh & Boyd, 2014):

\[ \Psi_\gamma(\theta)=\Psi(\theta)+\frac\gamma2\lVert\theta\rVert_2^2 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \ell_\gamma(z)=\min_u\Bigl[\ell(u)+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\lVert u-z\rVert_2^2\Bigr].\]
(B.10)

The gradient of \(\ell_\gamma\) is Lipschitz with constant \(1/\gamma\), and \(\ell_\gamma\) lies below \(\ell\) by at most \(\gamma/2\) times the largest \(\lVert\theta\rVert_2^2\) on \(\Theta\). The proximal losses apply Equation B.10 to cross-entropy in the scores \(z\) and to distillation in the scaled scores \(z/T\). The single-label smooth hinges add the quadratic to the competitor weights \(p\) only, so they are Moreau envelopes in the differences \(a\). They lie below the nonsmooth hinge by at most \(\gamma/(2k)\) (Lapin et al., 2016). The smooth multilabel hinge lies below by at most \(\gamma\).

Sparse hinge gradients. For a smooth hinge, the maximizer of Equation B.8 is the Euclidean projection of the scaled hinge terms \(h/\gamma\) onto \(\mathcal C\), with \(h_j=1+a_j\). The projection has a threshold \(\rho\) and a cap \(\kappa\), which is \(1/k\) on \(\Delta_k^\beta\), \(s^\star/k\) on \(\Delta_k^\alpha\), and absent on \(\Delta\) (Kiwiel, 2008; Lapin et al., 2015):

\[ p^\star_j=\min\bigl(\kappa,\;\max(0,\,h_j/\gamma-\rho)\bigr). \]

Every competitor with \(h_j\le\gamma\rho\) receives a zero gradient. Without smoothing, only the \(k\) largest competitors can have nonzero entries. Our kernels skip the zero entries on wide rows (Section 3.1).

\[ \ell(z,Y)=\max\Bigl(0,\,1+\max_{j\notin Y}z_j-\min_{y\in Y}z_y\Bigr). \]

\[ \gamma q_j+\log q_j=b_j+\gamma t_j-\nu.\]

For a given \(\nu\), each entry is a value of the Lambert \(W\) function (Corless et al., 1996).

As \(\gamma\to0\), the proximal losses approach cross-entropy and distillation. A larger \(\gamma\) bounds the gradient change between nearby scores.

#B.4 The SDCA solver

The SDCA solver of Section 1.2 trains linear and kernel classifiers with the losses of Table B.1 on fixed features, such as frozen embeddings. It works on the dual problem, whose variables are the gradient candidates of Appendix B.2, and stops on the duality gap.

Objectives. SDCA (Shalev-Shwartz & Zhang, 2013) maximizes the dual of the regularized training objective one example at a time. For \(X\in\mathbb R^{n\times d}\) with \(n\) examples, \(m\) classes, regularization \(C>0\), and convex losses \(\ell_i\) with Fenchel conjugates \(\ell_i^*\), the primal objective \(P(W)\) and its dual \(D(A)\) over \(A\in\mathbb R^{n\times m}\) are

\[ P(W)=\frac12\lVert W\rVert_F^2+C\sum_{i=1}^n\ell_i(W^\top x_i),\qquad D(A)=-\frac12\lVert X^\top A\rVert_F^2-C\sum_{i=1}^n\ell_i^*(-a_i/C),\]
(B.12)

with \(W=X^\top A\). Each step maximizes \(D(A)\) exactly over the row \(a_i\) of one example. For every loss of Table B.1, \(-a_i/C\) is a gradient candidate \(\theta\) of Equation B.7, and \(\ell_i^*\) is its \(\Psi\). A primal epoch costs \(O(ndm)\). Kernel SDCA replaces \(\lVert X^\top A\rVert_F^2\) with \(\mathrm{tr}(A^\top KA)\) for the Gram matrix \(K=XX^\top\). Its epoch costs \(O(n^2m)\), which is lower on wide data with \(d>n\).

Stopping criterion. Weak duality gives \(P(W)-P(W^\star)\le P(W)-D(A)\) for the minimizer \(W^\star\), so the duality gap bounds the suboptimality of \(W\). The solver evaluates \(P\) and \(D\) in fp64 with a first-order estimate \(e\) of their rounding error, and reports status == "solved" when

\[ P(W)-D(A)+e\le\mathrm{tol}\cdot\max\bigl(|P(W)|,\,|D(A)|\bigr).\]
(B.13)

A fit that reaches its epoch or time limit first returns another status, and its duality gap still bounds its suboptimality.

Coordinate updates. Hinge steps project onto the sets of Appendix B.2 in \(O(m\log m)\) (Kiwiel, 2008; Lapin et al., 2015, 2018). They combine active-set shrinking (Fan et al., 2008) with gap-safe screening (Ndiaye et al., 2017), while Equation B.13 is checked on the full problem. Entropy steps reduce to a scalar equation in the Lambert \(W\) function (Corless et al., 1996), which we solve with a bracketed Halley iteration.

#C.1 Tail underflow

A sum of unshifted exponentials can return zero for a cross-entropy gradient that the format can still represent. This happens on confident rows with many classes, where each term underflows on its own while their sum does not.

Versions 1 and 3 of Barron et al. (2026) give two listings of fixed CE (Listing C.1). Version 1 calls a library logsumexp, which shifts by the largest argument. Version 3 shifts by \(b=\max(0,\max_ja_j)\). On a correct prediction every difference \(a_j\) is negative, so \(b=0\) and the terms \(e^{a_j}\) are summed without a shift.

Take a row \(z=[0,-g,\dots,-g]\) with \(m\) classes, and let \(2^{-q}\) be the smallest subnormal (Table C.1). Every unshifted term \(e^{-g}\) rounds to zero once \(g\ge g_0=(q+1)\ln 2\). The exact sum \((m-1)e^{-g}\) and the target gradient underflow only at a margin larger by \(\ln(m-1)\):

\[ g\ge g_0+\ln(m-1).\]
(C.1)

Between the two margins, the unshifted sum returns zero for a gradient that the format can represent. This band widens with the logarithm of the number of classes (Figure C.1). Only in fp16 does it lie at margins that training normally reaches. This applies where the loss itself runs in fp16, as in JAX fp16 pipelines and in models cast with .half(). Under torch.autocast on CUDA, PyTorch computes F.cross_entropy in fp32 even for fp16 logits, so the band lies at the far larger margins of fp32. Close to \(g_0\), the unshifted terms are already rounded to a few subnormal steps, so the error of Version 3 grows before its first zero (Figure 2).

Our kernels shift the sum by the largest competitor, so its largest term is one and the sum cannot underflow. They merge the sums of wide rows in fp64 and keep a few fp64 numbers per row for the backward pass. The backward pass reads the logits once more, applies the upstream scale (Appendix C.2), and rounds each gradient entry once. The shift comes before every exponential and the scale before the only rounding. The gradient therefore keeps every entry that the format can represent, until the exact gradient underflows (Table C.4).

#C.2 Premature rounding under loss scaling

A fused kernel can lose most fp16 gradient entries if it rounds them before it applies the upstream scale: the gradient that the loss layer receives in the backward pass, such as a loss scale or a loss weight. Mixed-precision training scales the loss so that small gradients survive rounding in fp16 (Micikevicius et al., 2018), but only if the scale comes before the rounding. A kernel that stores the unscaled gradient \(x\) in the dtype of the logits computes

\[ \mathrm{fl}\bigl(\sigma\cdot\mathrm{fl}(x)\bigr)\quad\text{instead of}\quad\mathrm{fl}(\sigma\cdot x). \]

It returns zero for every entry below half the smallest subnormal and rounds every other entry twice. With a mean reduction, the entries of low-probability classes often fall into this range in fp16.

#C.3 Cancellation in hinge and distillation losses

Cancellation occurs when a computation subtracts two nearly equal numbers that were rounded before. The difference keeps only the rounding errors of its inputs, so its relative error can be as large as 1 or larger. Barron et al. (2026) show this for distillation near the teacher. We find a similar effect in the hinge terms near the unit margin.

Hinge terms near the unit margin. A hinge term \(h_j=1+z_j-z_y\) is small for a competitor close to the unit margin, which is where the hinge acts. The loss, the projection of Appendix B.2, and the sparse gradient all depend on these small terms. Two additions compute \(h_j\), and each one rounds. When one score is tiny and the other is close to 1, the first addition drops the low-order bits of the tiny score, and the second subtracts two nearly equal numbers. The result can be zero for a positive term. The competitor then drops out of the loss and its gradient, an effect similar to softmax collapse.

No order of the two additions avoids this on all inputs (Table C.3). Our kernels compute \(h_j\) with two chained TwoSum steps (Section 2.3) (Ogita et al., 2005). Each step returns a rounded sum and its exact rounding error. The two errors add up to everything that the rounded additions lost, so adding them back recovers the term up to the last rounding. This costs a few extra additions per term and no branches.

Distillation near the teacher. The distillation gradient \((p_s-p_t)/T\) subtracts two probabilities that are nearly equal when the student is close to its teacher. Automatic differentiation rounds both probabilities first, so the gradient is rounding noise even at \(z_s=z_t\), where it is exactly zero (Table 2). Following Barron et al. (2026), we compute the log-ratio \(r\) of the two probabilities from the logit difference \(d\) and form the difference with expm1, which is accurate for small arguments:

\[ p_{s,j}-p_{t,j}=p_{t,j}\,\mathrm{expm1}(r_j),\qquad r_j=\log\frac{p_{s,j}}{p_{t,j}}=d_j-\log\sum_ip_{t,i}\,e^{d_i},\qquad d=\frac{z_s-z_t}{T}.\]
(C.2)

No step subtracts two rounded probabilities. At \(z_s=z_t\), every \(d_j\) and \(r_j\) is zero, and every gradient entry is an exact zero.

#C.4 Reference tests

A loss that loses a gradient returns a plausible number, so the loss of accuracy shows only in a comparison with an exact value. We compare loss values and gradient entries with high-precision references and sweep the margin to the end of each format.

Pass bound. Floating-point results are usually compared with a mixed tolerance \(|\hat x-x|\le\mathrm{atol}+\mathrm{rtol}\,|x|\), as in numpy.isclose and torch.testing.assert_close. A fixed absolute tolerance hides exactly the failures that we test for. With atol=1e-6, a gradient entry of \(10^{-9}\) that collapsed to zero passes. A purely relative tolerance fails in the subnormal range, where even the nearest representable value has a relative error up to 1. We therefore tie both terms to the output format, following the standard error model of floating-point arithmetic with gradual underflow (Higham, 2002, Section 2.1). Against a 100-digit mpmath reference \(x\), a returned value \(\hat x\) passes when

\[ |\hat x-x|\le (64\,\varepsilon+\delta)\,|x|+\varepsilon\,x_{\min}.\]
(C.3)

The relative term \(64\,\varepsilon\) leaves room for the few dozen roundings of a fused kernel. It stays below 1 in every format, so a collapsed or cancelled value with a relative error of 1 or more fails. The term \(\delta\) is zero for cross-entropy. It grows with the number of classes on the paths that sum without compensation (top-\(k\) cross-entropy, and distillation on CUDA), as the error bound of a plain sum does. The absolute term \(\varepsilon\,x_{\min}\) is the spacing of the subnormal numbers (Table C.1). A returned zero therefore passes only when the exact value is within about one subnormal step of zero.

Memory-bandwidth limit. A training call of cross-entropy reads the logits twice and writes the gradient once, as do x.amax(dim=1) and torch.neg(x, out=y) together. Their summed time is the memory-bandwidth limit (dashed in Figure 1a): no loss kernel with this memory traffic can be faster.

#D.3 Accuracy

Fast kernels are useful only if they are as accurate as the baselines on ordinary inputs. We therefore score the timed calls of Appendix D.2 against 100-digit mpmath references.

Inputs. A 100-digit reference of a wide row can take hours, so we score 127 training configurations. They cover every loss with a framework baseline in bf16 and fp32, mostly on \(4096\times32{,}000\), \(16{,}384\times1000\), and \(2560\times50{,}257\) logits. From each timed input we draw 64 rows with a fixed seed and score the gradients of libsdca and of each baseline on these rows. We leave out loss values. libsdca returns them in fp32 for 16-bit logits, as PyTorch does under torch.autocast, which would favor it over eager F.cross_entropy and Optax.

Errors. The error of a gradient entry is its relative error in units of the machine epsilon \(\varepsilon\) of the storage format, and the error of a row is the largest one among entries with a normal exact value. A second metric divides the largest absolute error of a row by its largest exact entry. A collapsed entry is zero where the exact value is not, and a spurious entry is nonzero where it is zero. No libsdca output has a nonfinite, collapsed, or spurious entry.

Verdicts. Each comparison pairs libsdca with one baseline and receives the first of these verdicts that applies:

  • Less accurate. libsdca has a nonfinite, collapsed, or spurious entry that the baseline does not, or in either metric its largest row error exceeds that of the baseline by both a factor of 1.25 and one unit \(\varepsilon\).
  • More accurate. In either metric, the median over rows of the error ratio of libsdca to the baseline is below 0.8, and a two-sided sign test is significant at \(q<0.05\) after a Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995).
  • Neither. All other comparisons, including ties.

The factor 0.8 is \(1/1.25\), so both verdicts need the same margin. The extra unit keeps differences at the level of rounding, such as \(0.4\,\varepsilon\) against \(0.2\,\varepsilon\), from counting as worse. The sign test needs no assumption about the distribution of the errors, which take few distinct values near the rounding level.

It has no biases, LayerNorm, or positional embeddings. Weights stay in fp32, and we cast the logits to the stated dtype right before the loss. We train for 10,000 full-batch AdamW epochs with learning rate \(10^{-3}\), \(\beta=(0.9,0.98)\), weight decay \(\lambda=1\), and \(\epsilon\in\{10^{-8},10^{-12},10^{-16}\}\) over seeds 0–⁠9.

Teacher-initialized distillation (\(a+b\bmod 53\)). Following Section 7 of Barron et al. (2026), we train a 2-layer ReLU MLP teacher of width 512 on a 50% split of \(a+b\bmod 53\) to 99.99% mean test accuracy over five seeds. An identical student starts from the teacher's weights and trains for 2,000 full-batch Adam steps at learning rate \(2\times10^{-3}\) and temperature \(T=4\) without weight decay. The MLP and the loss run in bf16, fp32, or fp64.

#E.5 Hinge hyperparameter sweeps

Section 4.2 shows that the smooth multiclass hinge groks \(a+b\bmod 31\) at margins near one. This raises two questions: which hyperparameters it depends on, and whether a larger loss margin would help. We sweep the weight decay \(\lambda\), the learning rate, and the loss margin around the setting of Section 4.2, with ten seeds per setting on a CPU. Every run first memorizes the training set, and its test accuracy stays near 47% for the first few hundred epochs. What follows depends on the weight decay (Figure E.1).

  • Weight decay is necessary. Without it, the hinge stays at the test accuracy of the memorized solution. So does unfixed cross-entropy without weight decay, which ends at the largest margins of our runs.
  • More weight decay makes the hinge generalize earlier. Below \(\lambda=0.3\), the test accuracy rises only slowly, and from \(\lambda=0.3\) on all or most seeds generalize.
  • A larger learning rate has the same effect. At the smallest learning rate, the hinge does not generalize within 10,000 epochs.
  • A larger loss margin does not help. Rescaling the hinge as \(r\,\ell(z/r)\) makes it vanish only from margin \(r\). Without weight decay, it raises the final margin to about \(r\) and leaves the test accuracy unchanged.
